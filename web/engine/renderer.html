A frame on the GPU (and the path tracer)

Every frame, the engine walks the world, decides what can be seen, and turns that into a list of draws. One backend (Vulkan on Linux and Windows) then records the whole frame into one command buffer: light lists, sky tables, shadow maps, reflection probes, a lit HDR scene, clouds, a handful of screen-space passes, ray-traced reflections near the camera, and a composite that tone-maps the result onto the screen. The idea that holds it together is a small seam. The engine only ever says "draw this mesh with this material here, under these lights". Everything the GPU needs is the backend's business. This page is the map of the whole frame; the lights, the sky, the reflections, the clouds and the weather each have a page of their own.

How the physical renderer fits together

In November 2026 the Vulkan renderer took most of a "renderer realism" plan in a few days: thousands of real lights instead of 32, a sky computed from a model of the atmosphere, an eye that adapts to the frame, reflection probes along the streets, ray-traced reflections close to the camera, and clouds placed by a simulated weather. Each is its own system with its own page. They meet in one place: the shading of one pixel in mesh.frag (and in the two compute shaders that shade what a probe or a ray sees, probe_relight.comp and rt_reflect.comp, which include the same lighting code).

The meshes and materials come from the procedural generators and the bakes that cache them (the procedural language, bakes); a draw's class (structure, furniture, simBody…) is what the ray pick and the probe capture select by. The lights are data: kinds and pools in assets/scripts/lights.lua. Everything that gives off light publishes candidates on a board (the city's cars their head and tail lamps, buses their cabin lights, a streamed building its ceiling fittings, the flashlight, the beacons), and one picker in LightSystem chooses the frame's set within a budget the renderer states (Renderer::lightCapacity(): 31 on a forward renderer, 4,096 on clustered Vulkan; ADR-0362, 0363). DayNightSystem places the sun and the moon, reads their colour through the atmosphere model when the physical sky is on, publishes the weather and the wind, and runs the eye. The weather is simulated over the land and the sea (ADR-0390), and CloudMapSystem bakes from it the weather map that places every cloud (ADR-0386).

In the middle, each system turns into GPU work: froxel light lists, sky tables, a cloud march, probe cubes, a ray-tracing scene. On the right they are all read by one shader through the same set of bindings (set 0: the globals, the shadow cascades, the cluster buffers at 8–10, the sky view and aerial volume at 11–14, the sky cube at 15, the probes at 16–17, the weather map at 18; shaders/vulkan/globals.glsl), and lit by one function, shadeLight in lighting_common.glsl, which the probe relight and the ray hits include as well. That sharing is the point: a lamp lights a wall, the wall's probe and a ray's hit on the wall with the same maths (ADR-0373).

Two switches decide what you see. Clustered lights are Vulkan's default (RT_CLUSTERED=0 is the forward A/B). The physical sky is not the default yet: DayNightSystem turns it on only with RT_SKY=physical, and without it the frame keeps the hand-made day/night palette, the old night exposure (×6) and the analytic sky light. The sky view, aerial volume and sky cube then do not run, mesh.frag never reads a probe, and the rays stay off, because only the physical branch takes the probe's share out where the rays put theirs (ADR-0376). ADR-0369 lists what is left before physical becomes the default (the lit windows' calibration, the stars).

Vulkan only, so far. All of this is in the Vulkan backend. Metal and WebGPU loop a forward 32 lights (the picker trims to them by importance) and have none of the rest; Metal's own, older scattering sky is opt-in per level and tuned to the old exposure (ADR-0367). docs/renderer-parity.md, re-surveyed as a Vulkan | Metal | WebGPU ledger on 2026-11-07 (3b898f89, "Vulkan is the reference now"), is the list of what they lack; issue #203 tracks Metal parity for the physical renderer and the clouds.

Where it started: a CPU path tracer

The repository's first commit (d165c80b, 2026-06-01) was not a game engine. It was an offline path tracer: a program that makes a picture by firing many random rays per pixel, bouncing each one around the scene and averaging the light the bounces collect. "Cornell box with path tracing, glass, mirror, KD-tree, and bilateral denoising", standard library only. The interactive Metal renderer arrived the same day (3ba3ba60, "Add interactive Metal renderer with RHI abstraction and PBR shading"). Everything else on this page grew from that second commit, but the tracer is still in the tree and still builds: the raytracer CLI and the editor's in-process render both call one function, renderImage, which renders rows of the image as jobs on the job system.

The tracer is the reference the realtime renderer is held to. Its PBR material uses the same GGX BRDF as the GPU shaders (ADR-0017), and it applies the same fitted ACES curve, so an offline render and a viewer frame can be compared. Two pieces of it are worth knowing. First, the camera is a thin lens: each ray starts at a random point on the aperture disk and aims at the point on the focal plane, so depth of field falls out of the averaging for free.

Camera::generateRay: a thin lens with Brown distortion; lensRadius 0 is an exact pinhole src/camera.cpp
Ray Camera::generateRay(double u, double v, double lensU, double lensV,
                        double radialScale) const {
    // Brown radial distortion (+ the caller's per-channel scale) on centered
    // image coordinates; k1 = k2 = 0 and radialScale = 1 is exact passthrough.
    double s = 2.0 * u - 1.0;
    double t = 2.0 * v - 1.0;
    double r2 = s * s + t * t;
    double warp = radialScale *
                  (1.0 + lens.distortionK1 * r2 + lens.distortionK2 * r2 * r2);
    u = (s * warp + 1.0) / 2.0;
    v = (t * warp + 1.0) / 2.0;

    Vec3 target = lowerLeft + u * horizontal + v * vertical;

    Vec3 origin = position;
    if (lensRadius > 0.0) {
        double r = lensRadius * std::sqrt(lensU);
        double phi = 2.0 * PI * lensV;
        origin += r * std::cos(phi) * basisU + r * std::sin(phi) * basisV;
    }
    return {origin, normalize(target - origin)};
}

Second, the acceleration structures. The class is TriangleBVH (it was called KdTree until #117 wave 0): it splits the triangles into two halves at the median centroid of the longest axis, and each node keeps a box round its triangles. That makes it a median-split bounding volume hierarchy (BVH), not a space-splitting k-d tree. ADR-0041 added a second level for instancing: a MeshProto (one mesh, its own tree, a "BLAS", bottom-level acceleration structure) placed by many SceneInstances, with an InstanceBVH (the "TLAS", top level) over the instances' world boxes. A ray is moved into the instance's frame. The geometry is never copied into the world, so 600 street trees cost one tree's triangles.

The tracer's two-level query (src/scene.cpp, src/instance.cpp). The flat triangle soup and the instances live side by side, so scenes without instances render exactly as they did before ADR-0041.

Two tone curves. ADR-0037 gave the viewer a grade (contrast and saturation in scene-linear light) and a choice of ACES or AgX. One seam, four backends

ADR-0001 (2026-06-03) made a rule before there was a second backend: platform details live behind two seams, Window (windowing and input) and Renderer (the GPU). Renderer is a pure virtual class in src/renderer/renderer.h. It is not a command-buffer abstraction. It uploads resources and takes draw calls, and the backend builds and runs the whole pass graph inside endFrame(). Engine code never sees a Vulkan or Metal type.

Who talks to the seam, and who implements it. Line counts are the backend source files at the pinned commit (Vulkan's main file only; the ray-tracing scene is in rt_scene.cpp beside it); each backend also has its own shader tree (shaders/vulkan, shaders/metal, WGSL embedded).

    // here is CPU-side (one InstanceGroup vs. thousands of entities). A backend

                                   const std::vector<Mat4>& transforms,
                                   const RenderMaterial& material) {
        // (a backend without instancing: the bottom row's per-instance DATA -- a grass rank and its ground's
        // slope, a signal's state, read only by the Vulkan shaders -- must not reach its model matrix as a w)
        for (Mat4 m : transforms) { m.m[3][0] = m.m[3][1] = m.m[3][2] = 0; drawMesh(handle, m, material); }
    }
    // ...
    virtual void endFrame() = 0;

Notice the default drawMeshInstanced: a backend without instancing gets a loop of single draws. It also clears the matrix's bottom row first. That row is where the engine smuggles per-instance data (a grass clump's rank, a traffic signal's colour) to the Vulkan shaders, and it must not reach another backend as a perspective w.

The physical renderer widened the seam without changing its shape. Every addition is a virtual with a do-nothing default, so Metal, WebGPU and the null renderer compile unchanged and simply answer "no":

From the world to a command buffer

Application::renderFrame brackets a frame in three timed phases, named so a slow frame can say where it was slow (ADR-0077): acquire (beginFrame), encode (the systems' render calls walk the world and submit draws) and submit (endFrame, which on Vulkan records and submits the GPU's work).

The CPU half of a frame on Vulkan. With MAX_FRAMES_IN_FLIGHT = 2, the fence wait in endFrame is for the frame before last, so the CPU builds frame N while the GPU still draws N − 1. Everything read back (GPU times, the occlusion depth, the luminance the eye adapts to) is therefore a frame or two old, and nothing waits for it.

The renderer is immediate mode for the draw list: nothing about a draw survives the frame. Every frame the world is walked again, culled again, and every visible instance's matrix is written again into a per-frame, host-visible instance buffer. Meshes and textures do stay on the GPU; only the list of what to draw is rebuilt. That is simple and always correct, and it is also where the CPU's render time goes (see "What it costs"). The unit of the list is the DrawItem:

DrawItem: one queued draw, the backend's whole idea of "something to draw" src/renderer/vulkan/vulkan_renderer.cpp
struct DrawItem {
    MeshHandle mesh;
    MeshPush push;
    std::array<TextureHandle, 5> textures;   // albedo, MR, normal, AO, emissive
    float opacity = 1.0f;                     // < 1 → transparent pass (back-to-front)
    float sortPos[3] = {0, 0, 0};             // where it stands, for the transparent sort
    bool  terrain = false;                    // → terrainPipeline (CDLOD morph in vert)
    // INSTANCED (ADR-0097): the model matrices are rows [firstInstance, +instanceCount) of the
    // frame's instance buffer, read by the vertex shader at instance rate. drawMesh is a
    // one-instance draw; drawMeshInstanced is one draw for the whole visible set.
    uint32_t firstInstance = 0, instanceCount = 1;
    // WHERE IT IS: a world bounding sphere round every instance (radius < 0: unknown, always drawn). The shadow
    // cascades cull against it -- each cascade used to draw the whole frame's queue (the island: 9.7 ms of GPU,
    // three times the recording)
    float worldCenter[3] = {0, 0, 0};
    float worldRadius = -1.0f;
    // a GPU crowd kind: its instances are crowd.comp's (crowdInst) and its count an indirect command (crowdCmds)
    bool crowd = false;
    uint32_t crowdCmd = 0;
};

MeshPush is the 120-byte push-constant block the shaders read: albedo, metallic, roughness, emission, the material flags (a 32-bit word of switches like FLAG_ALPHA_TEST, FLAG_GRASS, FLAG_LOD_BAND), the procedural surface id (brick, asphalt, water…) and 16 floats of material features. Textures are bound per draw from a descriptor pool that is reset every frame. Since ADR-0372 a draw can also be routed elsewhere: between beginProbeFace and endProbeFace the same drawMesh calls go into a probe face's own queue (at most two faces a frame; a face with no slot left is dropped, which is why drawMeshInstanced now checks that its first draw was queued at all).

The pass graph

A pass is one trip through the GPU's pipeline that writes some images; later passes read them. The order and the image formats are fixed in recordCommandBuffer. The matrix below is the whole frame: one row per pass in the order they run, one column per image or buffer, W where a pass writes it and R where it reads it. The brackets on the left are the RT_GPU_TIMES buckets the timings come back in. Since October the frame has grown from eleven rows to seventeen, and almost all of the new ones run before the scene pass: they make the things a pixel reads while it is shaded.

The Vulkan frame at commit e55dfaaa, from recordCommandBuffer and the descriptor writes that feed each pass. The sky tables, the probes and the rays are only read under the physical sky (RT_SKY=physical); the sky tables are also only made then. The probes are still captured and relit, and the ray scene still built, under the old sky. The composite reads the DOF image instead of the HDR one when depth of field is on, and reads depth and normals for the underwater view and the debug views. The clouds' own half-resolution targets and history are left out.

Each record… call decides for itself whether it has anything to do this frame: the sky passes return at once unless the physical sky is on, the probe passes when no face is queued or no probe is placed, the ray pass unless raysThisFrame() says yes. So the code always reads as the full frame, and a switch only empties a stage.

Each pass in a sentence or two

PassWhat it does, and why it is there
cluster_bin.compThe clustered lights: the view is cut into 16 × 9 screen tiles × 24 depth slices (0.5 m to 2 km, exponential), the froxels, and every light is listed in each froxel its sphere reaches, at most 64 a froxel, the nearest its middle kept. One workgroup a froxel, 64 threads; it replaced a CPU binning that took 6.8–12.6 ms with 1,000 lights near the camera (ADR-0361, 0364). A pixel then loops only its own froxel's list. Lights.
Sky tablesOnly under the physical sky. sky_view.comp marches the atmosphere into a 192 × 108 dome from the transmittance and multiple-scattering tables the CPU uploads (setSkyTables); aerial_volume.comp marches the air for 32 × 32 screen tiles at 32 distances out to 120 km; sky_cube.comp and sky_prefilter.comp make a 128² cube with GGX-blurred mips for reflections. The sky's diffuse light is nine spherical-harmonic numbers made on the CPU (ADR-0367, 0370, 0371). Sky, atmosphere and exposure.
crowd.compThe city's far travellers (the "GPU crowd") are placed by a compute shader from their route and clock, writing their instance matrices and an indirect draw count. It runs before the passes that draw them.
Shadow cascadesCascaded shadow maps: the view is cut into up to four depth slices, and each gets its own 2048² depth map rendered from the sun, so near shadows are sharp and far ones still exist. The boxes are fitted round the camera (cascade_fit.h), not the view direction, so turning does not make shadows shimmer. Each cascade draws only the items whose sphere overlaps its four sides; grass, overlays, the camera-facing impostor cards and sprites cast nothing. Alpha-cut leaves use a second pipeline that cuts the shadow by the leaf texture's alpha. (The clouds' shadow is not a map: it is read from the weather map in the shader, below.)
Probe captureAt most two faces a frame of a reflection probe, a small cube camera placed along the streets (or on the storey you stand in). A face is 64² and stores what the surfaces are (albedo and metallic, normal and roughness, emission and distance), not how they are lit, through capture twins of the mesh pipelines. So the time of day costs no recapture (ADR-0372, 0374, 0380). Reflections.
Probe relightUp to four probes a frame lit from their stored surfaces as the scene pass lights a surface (the same shadeLight, the sun's cascades and the clouds' shadow), diffuse only, into a 64-probe cube array with five GGX mips (ADR-0373, 0374).
Ray scene buildOn a GPU with ray queries (the renderer asks for Vulkan 1.2 since ADR-0375): a bottom-level acceleration structure per nearby mesh, built nearest first and spread over frames, and a top level over the near instances rebuilt every frame, inside a budget of 25% of the free device memory and never over 1.5 GB (ADR-0376, 0381).
Scene passThe sky first (a full-screen triangle from sky.frag: the day/night palette and the sun disc, or under the physical sky the sky view with the sun through the air), then the geometry, sorted by kind into opaque (back-face culled, then two-sided), terrain (its own vertex shader, which morphs CDLOD tiles), water, transparent (sorted back to front, premultiplied alpha), and overlays (depth off). All lit by mesh.frag (next section). It writes three images: HDR colour, already multiplied by the camera's exposure (pre-exposed, ADR-0366, so fp16 holds noon and moonlight alike); a G-buffer (RGBA8: the octahedral world normal, the roughness, and the surface's reflection weight, which the rays use to replace exactly what the probe gave, ADR-0376); and depth. Depth is reverse-Z: near is 1 and far is 0, which with a float depth buffer keeps precision nearly even out to kilometres.
CloudsVolumetric clouds marched at half resolution against the scene depth, each cloud placed by the weather map (ADR-0386), blended into a reprojected history to hide the march's noise (ADR-0328, 0379), then upsampled bilaterally (edge-aware, so cloud does not bleed over a roof line) onto the HDR image. They go in before the post chain, so bloom and SSR see the clouded sky. Clouds.
lum_histogram.compA 64-bin histogram of the frame's log luminance, every fourth pixel, copied out and read when its frame slot's fence passes. Under the physical sky, DayNightSystem's eye exposes the next frames by the mean between its 35th and 95th percentiles, and keeps the brightest 3% from washing out (ADR-0368, 0378).
BloomThree half-resolution draws: a soft-threshold bright pass (its threshold in pre-exposed units), then a horizontal and a vertical blur. The composite adds the result.
SSAOScreen-space ambient occlusion: darkening creases and contact points by testing a hemisphere of samples round each pixel against the depth buffer. Half resolution, then a blur.
SSRScreen-space reflections: each pixel's reflection ray is marched against the depth buffer; where it hits, the HDR colour there is the reflection, with a confidence that fades at screen edges and on rough surfaces. This is what puts the mountains in the lake below.
Ray-traced reflectionsUnder the physical sky, on a GPU with ray queries: every pixel smoother than roughness 0.3 within 150 m traces its reflection against the near scene (rt_reflect.comp, at SSR's half resolution). A hit is shaded from its own triangle with shadeLight, the sun's shadow by a second ray; a miss takes the probe. rt_denoise.comp filters it and mutes SSR where the rays answer, rt_apply.comp adds it to the HDR image (ADR-0376). Measured: +0.56 ms of GPU.
Occlusion reduceThe renderer's first compute pass: depth reduced to 16 px tiles for the CPU's occlusion culling (its own section below).
DOFDepth of field, a thin-lens circle of confusion gathered at full resolution. Skipped unless the camera has a real aperture.
CompositeOne full-screen draw into the swapchain: lens distortion and chromatic aberration, then AO (with a floor), SSR mixed in by its confidence, the underwater view (lit, under the physical sky, by the light coming down into the water, ADR-0383), bloom, the grade and the tone map (ACES or AgX). Lighting: one BRDF, one set of units

ADR-0017 (2026-06-10) found the lighting in two mismatched halves. Light from the sky (image-based lighting) was proper GGX physically based shading, but the sun and lamps were Blinn-Phong with no 1/π, about three times too hot, and shadows only darkened the direct term. Under a bright sky a fully shadowed pixel kept about 80% of its brightness. The fix had two commitments. One BRDF for everything: Cook-Torrance GGX specular (the GGX distribution, height-correlated Smith visibility, Schlick Fresnel), Lambert/π diffuse, and inverse-square falloff with a windowed range for lamps. And artistic shadow controls that act on visibility, so they hold whatever the shading model.

Everything in November kept that commitment and made it literal. The BRDF moved out of mesh.frag into lighting_common.glsl (ADR-0373), so the scene pass, the probe relight and the ray hits include the same file, and "the same light" is the same code wherever a surface is lit.

// THE LIGHT ON A SURFACE (ADR-0372): the GGX terms, the lights' falloff, the sun's cascaded and the mountains' shadow,
// and one light's contribution -- shared by mesh.frag and probe_relight.comp (include after globals.glsl).

float distributionGGX(float NdotH, float a2) {
    float d = NdotH * NdotH * (a2 - 1.0) + 1.0;
    return a2 / max(PI * d * d, 1e-6);
}
float visibilitySmithGGX(float NdotV, float NdotL, float a2) {
    float gv = NdotL * sqrt(NdotV * NdotV * (1.0 - a2) + a2);
    float gl = NdotV * sqrt(NdotL * NdotL * (1.0 - a2) + a2);
    return 0.5 / max(gv + gl, 1e-5);
}
vec3 fresnelSchlickVec(float cosTheta, vec3 f0) {
    return f0 + (1.0 - f0) * pow(clamp(1.0 - cosTheta, 0.0, 1.0), 5.0);
}
float distanceAttenuation(float dist, float range) {
    float ratio2 = (dist * dist) / max(range * range, 1e-4);
    float window = clamp(1.0 - ratio2 * ratio2, 0.0, 1.0);
    return window * window / max(dist * dist, 1e-4);
}

The sky's light and the reflections

Everything that is not a direct light is the environment: the sky's diffuse light, and what a surface reflects. Here the two skies differ. Under the default palette sky the light is analytic: the shader evaluates the procedural sky (or an HDR equirect) by normal for the diffuse fill and along the reflection for specular, then applies the split-sum BRDF lookup table baked at start-up (brdf_lut.frag). Under the physical sky (ADR-0367..0371) the diffuse fill is the whole sky's irradiance from nine spherical-harmonic coefficients, and the reflection comes from a chain, each link a fallback for the next:

Instancing: one draw for a forest

An instanced draw draws one mesh many times in one call, each copy with its own matrix. The engine's InstanceGroup component (one mesh, one material, a list of world matrices) existed from early on, and Metal batched it. Vulkan did not: it drew one vkCmdDrawIndexed per transform with the matrix in a push constant, so every tree and grass patch cost a draw call. ADR-0097 made every Vulkan mesh draw read its model matrix per instance from the frame's instance buffer (binding 1, locations 5 to 8 in mesh.vert). A plain drawMesh is a one-instance draw: one path, not an instanced twin of every pipeline.

Two things decided the shape. Terrain's CDLOD tiles use the tangent slot for their morph target, a position, so a mesh says so (RenderMesh::tangentIsData) and keeps the full layout. The ADR is explicit that this is declared, not guessed from tangent length, because generators emit raw edge vectors 3 to 5 m long. Packing moved off the render thread. The octahedral encode costs more than a float copy, and packing on the render thread doubled a streamed detail cell's commit (11 → 24 ms). So Renderer::prepareMesh packs on any thread with no device calls and uploadPrepared only copies; the residency worker prepares building cells. A detail cell then committed in 1.7 ms (slowest 5.4). On metro_planned the layout saved 641 MB; frames match the full layout to within 1/255 (ADR-0096). Occlusion culling

Occlusion culling skips things hidden behind other things: the forest behind a hill, the city behind the building in front of you. Frustum culling alone cannot do that. ADR-0100 does it on the CPU against a recent frame's depth, with no waiting.

ADR-0100's loop. The depth is two to three frames old when it is read, so nothing ever waits on the GPU for it; the test is built to be conservative about that age.

Levels of detail: impostors and tier crossfades

A level of detail (LOD) is a cheaper stand-in drawn when something is far away. The engine has two families, and both swap without a visible pop using the same trick.

Forests: models near, impostors far (ADR-0129, 0193, 0195)

An impostor is a flat card carrying a picture of the real object. ADR-0129 drew far trees as two crossed side cards and a top card, merged per 256 m cell (one draw per cell), with near trees instanced per 512 m cell. ADR-0193 replaced the crossed cards with many-view impostors: each tree variant is baked from 8 directions round it at 3 elevations (0°, 35°, 70°) into an atlas; the card turns to face the camera, and the fragment shader blends the four nearest baked views by the camera's direction in the tree's frame. A card that turns to the camera cannot cast a sensible shadow, so the shadow pass skips it and two shadow-only crossed cards cast it instead. ADR-0195 then measured recipe_forest at about 27 fps and counted about 120 million triangles a frame, and the shadow pass again; impostors from 150 m and thinner needle cards took the GPU frame from 36.7 to 10.8 ms.

The dither band

Crossfading two opaque models with alpha blending would need sorting and would double the cost. Instead FLAG_LOD_BAND gives a draw a distance band and dithers it: across the band each pixel is kept or discarded by a 4×4 Bayer threshold (a fixed pattern of 16 thresholds that reads as a fine screen door). The outgoing model keeps pixels under its share, the incoming one pixels above; because the tests are complementary, every pixel goes to exactly one of them.

Left: which pixels each tier keeps when each holds half. Right: the city tier crossfade drives the share by time, not by distance.

mesh.frag: the band test, complementary for the tier going out and the one coming in shaders/vulkan/mesh.frag
    // FLAG_LOD_BAND (bit 19, ADR-0129): drawn only inside a distance band, dithered at its edges, so a
    // near model and its far impostor crossfade per pixel (features[3] = in0, in1, out0, out1)
    if ((pc.surfaceFlags.y & (1u << 19)) != 0u) {
        const vec4 b = pc.features[3];
        const float d = distance(inWorldPos, g.cameraPosition.xyz);
        // a 4x4 Bayer threshold: the band reads as a fine screen-door, not noise
        const float bayer[16] = float[16](0.0, 8.0, 2.0, 10.0, 12.0, 4.0, 14.0, 6.0, 3.0, 11.0, 1.0, 9.0, 15.0, 7.0, 13.0, 5.0);
        const ivec2 q = ivec2(gl_FragCoord.xy) & 3;
        const float thr = (bayer[q.y * 4 + q.x] + 0.5) / 16.0;
        // COMPLEMENTARY edges: a fade-OUT keeps the pixels under its threshold, a fade-IN those above
        // (1 - thr), so a model fading out and its impostor fading in over the same band split every
        // pixel between them. Both testing the same threshold kept the same pixels and dropped the rest:
        // mid-band half the pixels showed neither -- a see-through ring of trees (review, #rendering).
        if (b.y > b.x && smoothstep(b.x, b.y, d) <= 1.0 - thr) discard;
        if (b.w > b.z && 1.0 - smoothstep(b.z, b.w, d) <= thr) discard;
    }

A see-through ring. The comment above records the bug that made the thresholds complementary: both tiers first tested the same threshold, so mid-band they kept the same pixels and dropped the rest, and half the pixels showed neither model. The test a_tier_crossfade_splits_every_pixel_between_the_two_tiers guards it now.

City tiers crossfade in time (ADR-0226)

A building cell (250 m) has three tiers: full detail, flat facades from 300 m, a mass-box stand-in from 950 m. The switch used to happen in one frame, a whole cell at a time. A spatial band would have drawn a cell's detail out to ~480 m and discarded most of it, about 2.5× the detail cells. So the fade runs over time: when a cell's distance test flips while it is on screen, RenderSystem fades it over 0.35 s and expresses the share as a band so wide (100 km) that the same share holds at every pixel of the cell. No shader change.

tierFadeBand: turn a share of pixels into the existing LOD band src/engine/systems/render_system.cpp
void tierFadeBand(RenderMaterial& m, double dist, float share, bool coming) {
    // the dither's share as a band so wide (kBand) that it reads the same at every pixel of a cell: smoothstep(e0,
    // e0 + kBand, d) = s at d = dist when e0 = dist - t kBand, t = smoothstep^-1(s). Coming in keeps the pixels the
    // band's fade-in keeps (a share `share`), going out those its fade-out keeps: complementary thresholds
    // (mesh.frag), so a pair at shares a and 1 - a covers every pixel once.
    constexpr double kBand = 1.0e5;
    auto inv = [](double s) { return 0.5 - std::sin(std::asin(1.0 - 2.0 * s) / 3.0); };
    m.flags |= RenderMaterial::FLAG_LOD_BAND;
    const double e0 = dist - inv(coming ? share : 1.0 - share) * kBand;
    if (coming) { m.lodIn0 = static_cast<float>(e0); m.lodIn1 = static_cast<float>(e0 + kBand); m.lodOut0 = m.lodOut1 = 0.0f; }
    else { m.lodOut0 = static_cast<float>(e0); m.lodOut1 = static_cast<float>(e0 + kBand); m.lodIn0 = m.lodIn1 = 0.0f; }
}

Measured on an island_8_nature flight (40 m up, 30 m/s): p50 13.9 / p99 24.6 ms with the fades, 14.0 / 24.8 without. RT_NO_TIER_FADE=1 pops as before; RT_TIER_FADE_S=6 slows a fade enough to see it in a screenshot. Vulkan only: Renderer::supportsLodBand() is false elsewhere, and without the band both tiers would draw whole at once.

Moving instances drawn between steps (ADR-0240)

The simulation runs at a fixed 60 Hz; the screen runs at whatever it runs. Entities are drawn between their last two steps' poses by the frame's interpolation alpha. Instance groups had no such thing, so the city's cars, baked into groups in the fixed step, froze on every frame that ran no step: at 75 fps, 60 of 300 frames (Glenn: "buses and cars sometimes shake. Their position is not stable. Trains actually appear very stable in contrast"). Trains were placed per frame, which is why they glided.

Now an InstanceGroup can name its instances (ids), keeps the last bake beside this one, and RenderSystem blends each named instance between them. A car body is keyed by its id; its glass, driver, riders and lamps take the body's id plus a part number, so they move as one.

How it got here

June: Metal first, the seam before the second backend. The path tracer and the Metal viewer shared the first day; ADR-0001 set the seams two days later. ADR-0017 unified the lighting on Metal and ADR-0037 found SSAO costing ~33 ms at full resolution (toggling it took the arena from 20 to 60 fps) and moved it to half resolution, where SSR already ran. On Vulkan today AO, SSR, bloom and the cloud march all run at half resolution. Vulkan (ADR-0057) and WebGPU (ADR-0058) followed at the end of June, each built in slices: clear screen, lit mesh, forward, shadows, post, instancing and terrain.

September 24: memory and draw calls. In one session: one allocator and upload queue (ADR-0094), residency (ADR-0095), the standard vertex (ADR-0096), instancing on Vulkan with the test forest that measures it (ADR-0097), and occlusion culling (ADR-0100). metro_planned's peak went from 9.6 to 3.6 GB. The common thread: each fix came with a measuring tool first (mem?, walk_probe.py, instancing_forest.json).

October: measuring the GPU itself. RT_GPU_TIMES (fcbdb2bf, 2026-10-05) showed shadows at 9.7 ms of a 14.1 ms GPU frame because every cascade drew everything; the per-cascade cull fixed it. Then the forests (ADR-0193, 0195), the city tier crossfade (ADR-0226, during the island streaming work of 2026-10-23) and moving instances (ADR-0240).

November: the renderer realism plan. On 2026-11-06 a plan (~/.claude/plans/renderer-realism.md) surveyed the Vulkan renderer and set an order: many lights, a physical sky, reflections, then materials, anti-aliasing, shadows, volumetrics and GI. Most of the first three landed on 2026-11-07 and 08, each measured before it was built.

Lights. Glenn asked about deferred rendering for "many, many light sources". ADR-0361 measured first: each light cost the forward scene pass about 0.1 ms, the same in a park full of overdraw as on a plain street, because the depth test already lit each pixel about once. Deferred's win was one the frame mostly had; what cost was how many lights each pixel looped. So: clustered forward, prototyped behind RT_CLUSTERED=1, with deferred kept for decals or a cheap G-buffer later. Then, after Glenn's "are we patching things because it's a quick win?", one light system with lights as data (ADR-0362), clustered by default (0363), binned on the GPU (0364), room lights from the fittings (0365).

The sky and the eye. One engine unit of light became 20,000 lux and the scene pre-exposed (ADR-0366); the Hillaire atmosphere on the CPU with tests, then on the GPU behind RT_SKY=physical (0367); auto exposure from a histogram (0368); lamps at their real candela and glows in real luminance (0369); the aerial perspective volume, after Glenn asked why the hazy mountains glowed (0370); the sky as a cube and nine harmonics (0371).

Reflections. Probes captured, relit and used (ADR-0372..0374), the probe lookup cut from 7.6 ms to 0.5 ms by a plan grid. Ray tracing measured before it was built (0375: everything would not fit on the island, what is within 150 m would), the renderer moved to Vulkan 1.2, and ray-traced reflections near the camera with clean fallbacks to the probes and the sky cube (0376), then interiors (0377, 0380), cars in the ray scene (0381), night windows and a room's bounce (0378, 0382, 0383), glows in the rays' units (0385).

Clouds and weather. The clouds, rebuilt over #187 (from ADR-0328 on 2026-11-05), were placed by a weather map, typed as data and given a shadow on the ground (0386–0388), organised round eddies (0389), and finally placed by a weather simulated over the land and the sea (0390).

All of it is Vulkan only. Issue #203 tracks Metal parity; the parity ledger has the clustered-lights row (c1529809) but still lists the probes and the physical sky as Metal-only rows from before this work.

What is still open, from the notes and ADRs: the physical sky is not yet the default (ADR-0369: the lit windows' calibration and the stars first), and with it the probes, the rays and the auto exposure stay opt-in; the probe relight and the ray hits light from the globals' 32 lights, not the froxel lists, so a storey's probe sees its fittings as glowing panels rather than light on its walls (ADR-0380, owed); no shadows from lamps (a shadow atlas is the light plan's phase 5); the draw list is rebuilt every frame (the render-profile note names retained static instance buffers and GPU-driven culling with indirect draws as the next lever); Metal and WebGPU lack everything Vulkan-only above; camera-relative rendering (ADR-0095) is planned but not built, which is why positions stay 32-bit floats in the vertex.
