How the City Thinks

The living-world scenes are full of cars and pedestrians that commute, queue at red lights, yield to each other, and occasionally fender-bender. Here is the machinery behind that — no machine learning, just perception, memory, and a few honest rules.

The layer cake: three altitudes of decision

Every agent — a driver or a walker — is a stack of three layers, each answering a different question. The split is the load-bearing design decision of the whole sim.

Strategic — where am I going?route

An A* search over the NavGraph — the directed lane graph derived from the procedural road network — produces a lane-accurate route for the current trip (a commute on a daily schedule, or a perpetual random wander in the lab level). This layer is just data: a polyline of lane centrelines to travel.

Tactical — what do I do right now?the agent

Perception + memory + a small state machine decide, on a slow think cadence, a target speed along that route: stop at the line, follow the leader, slow into the turn, yield to the person about to cross. This layer is the agent. It never touches a physics engine, which is why it runs identically headless in the test suite.

Motor — who moves the body?one authority per regime

Ambient traffic (the default) is integrated directly by the planner: position = path(s), s += v·dt — drawn as instanced meshes, collided via kinematic proxies. Interactive cars are promoted: when you commandeer an ambient car, the sim releases its agent and a real Jolt physics vehicle with the same body spawns in its place, driven through the same {throttle, steer, brake} seam the player uses.

The "one motion authority per regime" rule came from pain. An earlier design gave every ambient car two clocks — a kinematic planner ghost marching on rails and a physics body trying to chase it — and every fix was another reconciliation loop between them. Pedestrians, whose bodies simply execute what the plan proposes, worked all along. So each regime now has exactly one owner of motion: the planner for ambient traffic, physics for anything the player touches. This is also how ambient traffic works in shipped open-world games, for the same reason.

The cognition loop

Every thinking agent runs the same five-stage cycle; each stage is a small pure primitive in engine/ai.

  • Sense. A flat vision wedge in the ground plane plus a height band — 2.5D, because the city is grade-separated: a car crossing an overpass 5.8 m up must not read as "in front of me". Perception is imperfect by design: below reliability 1.0 an agent occasionally misses a sighting, deterministically from its seed.
  • Remember. Sightings become tracks with smoothed velocity estimates (successive sightings differenced + an EMA — a poor man's Kalman). A track decays over 4 seconds and, while unseen, extrapolates along its velocity: object permanence. A cap of 32 tracks (~1.5 KB per agent) evicts the weakest.
  • Predict. Constant-velocity closest-approach and disc-collision time from the tracked velocities. Dot products, not ML — a driver brakes when a predicted collision is less than 2 seconds away, which means it eases off before the crosser is in the lane, and keeps yielding about 3 seconds after losing sight of them.
  • Decide. A utility choice with hysteresis: the incumbent option keeps its seat unless a challenger beats it by a clear margin and a minimum hold has elapsed; an emergency (imminent collision) preempts instantly. Near-tied scores can flip forever without the behaviour ever flapping. Decisions are made on a slow, per-agent-staggered think clock (0.35 s) and held between thinks — a held answer reads as intent, a new answer every 100 ms reads as wigging out.
  • Act. The motor layer above: planner integration for ambient agents, the physics driver seam for promoted ones.

Drivers vs pedestrians

Drivers

Sees: a narrow forward cone — 18 m range, 0.45 rad (~26°) to each side: a crosser in the lane ahead, not someone on the far sidewalk. Deliberately does not treat other moving cars as cone obstacles — car-vs-car is handled by lanes, length-aware car-following, and the signals (braking for every car in a wide cone deadlocked junctions).

Thinks: senses every tick; re-decides on the 0.35 s think cadence. Personality: a per-agent pace factor in [0.85, 1.15] derived from the brain seed's bits, so the fleet doesn't move in lockstep.

Yields to: people (remembered tracks predicted into its corridor), red signals at a set-back stop line, the junction box, and oncoming traffic when turning.

Ring colours: green cruising · teal following · amber yielding · violet turning · red held.

Pedestrians

Sees: a short, wide cone — 4.5 m range, 1.2 rad (~69°) to each side: peripheral vision for a body that turns easily — plus 360° separation from anyone standing close.

Thinks: senses at think cadence (successive sightings a think apart are exactly what feeds the velocity estimator) and leans away from where each remembered neighbour will be 0.4 s from now, so two approaching walkers part early instead of flinching late.

Yields to: signals at the corner (walkers hold at the kerb, not a stop line), other walkers, signal poles, and the player — who gets a wide hard berth so near-misses read as step-arounds.

Ring colours: green walking · amber avoiding · red held (waiting at a crossing, or physically blocked).

The junction rulebook

Junctions are where flat rules stop being enough, and every rule below exists because its absence produced a visible failure:

  • The stop line is behind the crosswalk. A car holds at the junction-box radius plus the painted zebra band (0.5–3.6 m past the mouth) plus a margin plus half its own body length — so the bumper stops short of the crosswalk instead of legally "waiting" in the middle of the intersection looking like a red-light runner.
  • Signals: 12 s green, then all-red clearance. The cycle is green (12 s, time for a few queued turns) → yellow → 2.5 s of all-red so the box drains before cross traffic gets its green. A car already past the line when green expires always commits and clears — a clamped mid-box car is a trap for everyone.
  • Box occupancy. No car crosses its stop line while a crossing-heading car is inside the box. This is the rule that stopped mid-box wrecks from forming.
  • Turn yield. A turning car waits for live oncoming approach traffic, with agent-order right-of-way between two stopped opposing turners — otherwise both yield to each other forever.
  • Fender-benders, then recovery. Two cars meeting on a closing course don't ghost through each other: both freeze (red rings), roll their overshoot back out, and resume on staggered per-brain holds so the tangle unwinds. And an escape valve: a car wedged through more than 5 consecutive freezes ignores contact until it drives clear — the tow-truck resolution, since a crossing-path contact can never otherwise resolve without reverse gear.
