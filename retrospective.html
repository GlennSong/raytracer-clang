Three months in

A retrospective on the first 92 days — what the method got right, what it got wrong, and what is still broken.

92days
1,299commits

On June 1 this was a Cornell box with a KD-tree and a bilateral denoiser. On August 31 it is a procedurally generated city you can walk into a building in — three render backends, a physics vehicle fleet, an agent simulation, a Qt editor, a sky with a real moon and real light pollution, and an offline path tracer that still agrees with all of it.

100% AI-written

As on the about page: every line of this engine was written by AI, prompted from start to finish. So this is also a retrospective on that — what three months of AI-driven engineering actually produced, including the failure modes, which turn out to be specific and repeatable rather than mysterious.

The spine

June — the foundation blitz

45 decision records, nine of them on June 3 alone. Platform seams, fixed timestep, a sparse-set ECS, physics behind a wall, Lua as the one procgen authoring path — then the entire procedural substrate: L-systems, curves, CDLOD terrain, the road model, the city grammar. Almost all of it is still standing.

July — systems, and a city that had to use them

11 records: event bus, debug draw, profiler, audio, animation, glTF skinning. Underneath, the city stopped being a demo and became a simulation — agents with goals and perception, a car fleet, routing.

August — contact with reality

Continuous integration exists for the first time on August 5 — day 66 of 92. On August 23 the Vulkan backend runs on real hardware for the first time. The last week is the sky, the road network's joint vertical solve, the earthwork field, and doors that open.

The decision rate — 45, then 11, then 6 — is not simply maturity. Two of August's six exist only because this retrospective was being written: they were recorded retroactively, days after the code shipped. A third change of the same weight still has no record at all. And 21 of the 83 carry no date. Record-keeping decayed while the decisions got bigger.

What worked

The seams held — and we know, because the trigger fired

Every decision record ends with a revisit trigger: the condition that should reopen it. The very first one, on June 3, put platform code behind two seams and honestly recorded its own weakness — "the abstraction is unproven by a second backend" — with the trigger "adding a second backend, at which point validate the seam holds."

That trigger fired 81 days later. Six real bugs surfaced the first day Vulkan met a GPU: a descriptor-pool cap, back-face culling, a shader hash, a baked albedo being overwritten, a camera sign, a window plugin. Every one was inside the backend. None was a leak through the seam. The June bet paid — and it was checkable only because someone wrote down in advance what would prove it wrong.

Instrument before you fix

A junction on the test city had a road arriving at −78% grade into a +33.5% one, with a 9-metre cliff at the edge. Five different plausible explanations fit the screenshot, each justifying a different fix.

None was right. A new dump — every surface sampled at 1-metre stations from the junction — showed the three arms meeting the shared node at 51.32, 48.64 and 43.30 metres and named the real cause: each road solved its height profile alone, and nothing ever re-agreed the node they shared. The spread went to zero in one pass.

Measurements that refute their own plan

The terrain plan asserted that a new smoothing field "can only reduce" a certain class of error. The census disagreed — identical cell for cell, 134 → 134. Chasing that non-result found a different bug entirely: building foundations winning priority inside road footprints and lifting the ground above the road.

The instrument was independent enough to contradict the code that made it, and the disagreement was written into the commit rather than smoothed over.

Named reds beat silent greens

Some tests are deliberately left failing, with the number, the cause and what is owed recorded at the point of failure. One gate reads 0.86 against a 0.55 limit — and the commit that caused it predicted 0.86 in its own message, listing the three variants measured first and the trade it bought: unnaturally steep banks beside roads cut from 2,041 to 950.

A red you predicted is a decision. A red you discover is a regression. Keeping those distinguishable is most of what makes a test suite worth running.

What went wrong

The verification gap

The largest failure, and structural rather than any single bug.

  • No CI until day 66. Three months of "it compiles" was, mostly, a claim about one machine.
  • The Vulkan backend shipped four phases having never been compiled. The first real compile found a reserved-keyword shader bug, a wrong uniform field, and 43 broken logging calls. Review had passed all of it.
  • Linux CI was red on arrival and stayed red, which turns a build signal into noise. The list of known failures then decayed in both directions: one listed failure was fixed in passing and nobody noticed, another went green unremarked, and a new one appeared without being added.
  • "Written here, verified there" was the editor's operating mode for three months — the shell compiled on Linux while only a Mac could see a pixel.

The through-line: this project got very good at proving things it measured, and had almost no defence against anything nothing was pointed at.

Documentation decays silently, in every direction

All of this was live in the repository when the retrospective was written:

  • 21 of 83 decision records carry no date.
  • The editor plan said "skeleton done" for two and a half months while the editor grew a working viewport, two native panels and its own test suite.
  • The tech-debt entry tracking platform failures named the wrong two tests.
  • The context file every session reads first gave a build command that cannot work — it collides with the offline renderer's own output directory and fails outright. It sat there through the entire Linux port.

None of it was caught by reading. All of it was caught by someone acting on it and hitting a wall.

Prose does not hold

The project's own retention notes document four cases where correct, written-down knowledge — in the standing rules file, in a decision record with measurements attached — was read and violated anyway, and a subsystem was rebuilt worse. The rules file is ~400 lines, and every line added lowers the odds any single line is honoured.

What survived instead were conventions with a test attached. Not because they were better documented — because breaking them makes something fail.

Knowledge survives only in a form that can fail. Everything else is a hope with a timestamp.

Still open, honestly

  • One freeway drive test has failed on Linux since CI began, is byte-identical before and after three months of road work, and remains unexplained.
  • A terrain fixture and the real city disagree, and nobody knows why — the same change makes the synthetic test worse while halving the defect in the shipped city. The fixture keeps its original contract until that is understood.
  • Building foundations still win priority inside road footprints: 86 of 134 measured cases. Found by accident, not yet fixed.
  • The stars, the moon's phases and the light-pollution glow are verified on one backend only; the other two are mirrored but uncompiled.

The one-sentence version

The method — instrument first, measure the trade, name the red, write the revisit trigger — is genuinely good and produced a lot of correct work fast. Its failure mode is just as consistent: anything no instrument was pointed at drifted, from a shader that had never been compiled to a build command in the file every session reads first.
